# Python DSL Survey for SHOCCS: Editor's Report

Status as of 2026-10-12. Branch `worktree-python-dsl-survey`, base commit 4895b5a. This report consolidates four parts, one fact sheet and one cross-cutting note under `docs/python-dsl-survey/` (document map in Section 8). The C++ figures in Sections 1 and 2 were measured for this report on 2026-10-12 inside the devcontainer (Part IV, `05-measurements.md`); every other number is taken from the parts. Section 7 says which figure was kept where the parts disagreed.

## Executive summary

The maintainer asked three questions. The short answers follow, then the recommendation.

**Which idioms must be handled efficiently?** Part I catalogues 51 idioms (I01 to I51; 29 in the time loop). Every hot idiom reduces to seven kernel shapes (P0 to P6, `01-codebase-idioms.md` Section 4), none of which needs a general sparse matrix, atomics, team scratch or a library solver. Cut-cell "unstructuredness" enters the runtime in exactly six places (Section 2.4), and all six reduce at assembly time to O(|R|) tables around a regular axis stencil. At 256^3 with a sphere (16.78M points, about 6.5% solid, so about 15.7M fluid points) each direction has about 400k closure rows (2.4% of D) and about 15.3M interior rows with a shared stencil, about 91% of D rows and about 97% of fluid rows; the irregular gather-dot-scatter touches 0.5 to 2.5% of points (Part I Section 7). The only blocker is I35, the per-point Lua callback, which every option replaces with SymPy-generated array expressions.

**Which Python packages fit, and how are our idioms expressed or left out?** Part II surveyed 18 options with an independent verification pass: 164 claims after the recount (originally 161 = 11 Apple fact-sheet claims + 150 framework claims): 99 confirmed, 7 refuted, 58 uncertain; 244 including the nine comparators (144/12/88), which were added in this pass (Section 3). No option scores well on both the fp64 CPU bar and Apple GPU: the best CPU candidates (Numba 9, Devito 8.5, DaCe, Halide and Mojo 8) score 0 to 4 on Apple GPU, and the best Apple access (direct Metal 8, MLX 6.5) has no fp64 CPU path. All express our idioms the same way (Part II Section 5) and leave out the same eight things (Section 6), among them the Lua callback and Apple fp64. The shortlist is a set of roles: Numba (loop-form CPU emitter), JAX (array-form CPU/CUDA emitter; with Devito one of the two options whose local small-2D measurements fall below the C++ figure, Devito's being serial and against an estimate), NumPy/SciPy assembled CSR (oracle and table producer), direct Metal (fp32 or df64, gated on a precision study). PyTorch, Halide, Devito and DaCe are second tier. A host measurement made today (Part IV D, a synthetic SHOCCS-shaped RK4 step on the owner's M4) adds the datapoint the survey lacked. A fused fp64 step on the CPU from Python (eager NumPy 0.10 ms, compiled MLX CPU 0.085 ms, torch eager 0.15 ms at 51x51) matches the discretization bar of four C++ RHS submits (91 us) and is far under the shipped 0.90 ms step; at 65^3 the best Python CPU fp64 paths (4.2 to 4.5 ms) are within 1.4x of eight-thread Kokkos RHS submits (3.2 ms) and under one-thread Kokkos (7.5 ms), so CPU parity is plausible but not yet shown in 3D or with real coefficients. The Apple GPU in fp32 is held at 0.35 to 0.5 ms per step by a 167 to 181 us sync floor at the shipped 2D sizes, where it loses to the CPU, and is 4 to 6x faster than the best CPU fp64 path at 129^3 and 257^3.

**What would it take to build our own DSL?** Part III developed five designs to one template and had three judges score them. Each states its effort in its own unit and to its own endpoint, so the figures are not comparable: D3 6 to 8 calendar months (Euler-capable, CPU and Metal), D2 7 to 8 months (CPU, Metal, Euler, CUDA), D5 7 to 9 developer-months (Metal and Euler), D1 8 to 12 person-months (its phase 4 only, not Euler), D4 10 to 13 engineer-months (Euler on CPU plus Metal df64), plus 10 to 25% of an FTE ongoing for the compiler designs. D5 is not the low end of that range; the judges still ranked it, the no-compiler "thin waist", first unanimously (171 of 225; the other four lie within 6.5 points, inside rubric noise), because its central claim can be tested in weeks of C++ against the live Kokkos RHS.

**Recommendation.** Adopt the Part III hybrid (`03-dsl-designs.md` Section 9): keep the C++ mesh, stencils and assembly loops; change only the assembly sinks to export compact tables and the exact operator over a packed vector; add a nanobind module and a Kokkos backend of ten primitives (B1 to B11, B4 absorbed into B3; B labels are backend primitives, P0 to P6 Part I's kernel shapes), one of them the fused RK stage kernel B11; replace Lua with a NumPy plus SymPy front end; build no compiler now. Treat Apple GPU as a gated phase 4 in fp32 or hand-written double-float, because no Apple GPU has fp64 and no framework offers an emulated dtype (Part IV D measures the fp32 GPU at 0.35 to 0.5 ms per synthetic step at the shipped sizes against 0.085 ms on the CPU, and 4 to 6x ahead of the CPU at 129^3 and 257^3). The C++ measured in the devcontainer (Section 1) puts the shipped heat.lua step at 0.90 ms, 59% of it host manufactured-solution evaluation, against an RHS graph of 22.7 us; every design compared itself against that inflated bar, and the fixed-up step is estimated at about 0.15 ms. Adjustment after the host measurement: phase 1 gains a parallel array arm (the real exported tables through the `host_primitives_bench.py` harness with NumPy, compiled MLX CPU and torch.compile CPU, under the same gate (2) as the C++ fused-stage arm); if it passes with real tables, the Kokkos CPU backend becomes optional and the thin waist shrinks to C++ assembly plus nanobind export plus a Python runtime. The most valuable next action is still to build natively and measure the fixed-up C++.

## 2. Findings that constrain the decision

### 2.1 fp64 on Apple GPUs

No Apple GPU (M1 to M5) has hardware fp64 and MSL has no usable `double`; PyTorch MPS 2.14.1 raises on float64; MLX runs fp64 on CPU only (request #1905 closed as not planned); jax-metal is dead; Taichi's and Halide's Metal backends have no f64; Kokkos 5.x has no Metal backend; AdaptiveCpp's SYCL-to-Metal backend is experimental, macOS 26 only, no `double`. All confirmed in the `00-apple-gpu-facts.md` log.

Consequences. "MPS like PyTorch" cannot mean fp64 for this solver; the options are fp32 or a hand-written double-float (df64) layer, which no framework offers as a dtype. Whether fp32 is accurate and stable for small-psi closures is open; psi is clamped to [1e-12, 1-1e-12] at `src/mesh/object_geometry.cpp:87-95` and E4_1 further clamps to [1e-4, 1-1e-4]. Part III Section 8.5 adds a point the designs missed: the shipped workloads (BO sweeps, stability scans) are ensembles of small 2D cases, not the 128^3 to 256^3 runs the designs optimise for, so a GPU pays here only by batching many independent small cases per dispatch, which nobody has measured with real tables; phase 4 carries an ensemble gate for it. Part IV D supplies the floor that gate has to amortise: on the M4 any GPU op plus synchronisation costs 167 to 181 us, so a whole-step-compiled fp32 step is 0.35 to 0.5 ms from 51x51 to 201x201 against 0.085 ms for the best CPU fp64 path at 51x51, and the GPU leads clearly only from 401x401 (0.57 against 1.49 ms). The fp32 error on the synthetic E2 problem is 1e-7 to 2.4e-7 relative after 10 steps; it says nothing about real small-psi closures.

### 2.2 Dispatch overhead at small sizes

At heat.lua scale the state is L2-resident and every kernel is launch-bound. The measured C++ launch floor is 0.55 us per tiny kernel at 1 thread; a step issues about 280 launches (Part I Section 5.2). The measured shipped step at 51x51 on 1 thread decomposes as 4 x (22.7 us RHS graph + 132 us fill_source + 33.9 us update_boundary) + 31.9 us stats = 786 us of the 900 us step; the remainder is slot copies, timestep logic, logging and the write call. So fill_source is 59%, update_boundary 15%, the four RHS submits 10%, stats 4% (`05-measurements.md` Section C).

So any Python route must compile the whole RK step, or K steps, into a handful of kernels with one readback per chunk. The two local whole-step figures below the C++ figures are JAX (step 66 to 74 us at 51x51; 1.7 ms at 64^3; 86 ms at 256^3) and Devito (7.2 us per fused step at 51x51, serial, against an estimate on synthetic tables); both were taken on the Mac host while the C++ ran in the container, and both must now be read against the fixed-up estimate of about 0.15 ms, not the shipped 0.90 ms. Part IV D measures the same shape of step on the same host (Section 1 table): 0.085 to 0.149 ms per fused fp64 CPU step at 51x51 against 91 us for four RHS submits; 4.2 to 4.5 ms at 65^3 against 3.2 ms (8 threads) and 7.5 ms (1 thread). Measured dispatch floors: 0.46 us (NumPy), 0.75 us (torch eager), 6.4 us (torch.compile CPU), 8.7 us (MLX CPU compiled), 167 to 181 us for any GPU op plus sync; eager MPS, at about a hundred kernels per step, costs 1.5 to 2.2 ms. CPU parity from Python is plausible, not yet shown in 3D or with real coefficients.

### 2.3 Setup versus runtime

Everything that depends on psi, BC type, wall-versus-object or interpolation is baked into coefficients once per (geometry, BCs, scheme, alpha); the runtime inputs are u and t (Part I Section 8). Setup today is wasteful at scale (derived estimates from Part I, not measurements): about 700k tiny Kokkos allocations at 256^3 from per-line Views (`src/matrices/block.hpp:30-94`; several per line over about 78.3k lines per direction), and about 1e9 comparisons per (r, dir) pair in the O(|R|)-per-r `interp_line` scan (`src/mesh/mesh.cpp:170-207`; about 1.3e5 `interp_line` calls per pair, each scanning up to about 12k records). All of it is fixable because setup only emits tables; psi, alpha, masks and coefficients should be runtime table arguments (graft G8) so the BO loop, which today runs the binary per parameter point through `cpp_bridge.py`, never recompiles.

Two runtime habits must go in any port, and should go in the C++ regardless: per-stage host evaluation of the manufactured solution over the whole field (about 14 full-field evaluations per step, 4 of them wasted in `update_boundary`; `src/systems/heat.cpp:296-354`), and per-call rebuild of object-BC gather lists (`src/mesh/mesh.hpp:97-115`). The first is now measured: fill_source alone is 59% of the shipped step at 51x51 (1 thread) and 72% at 65^3 (8 threads). With time-separable source terms hoisted into spatial factors (graft G6), the hot loop holds no transcendentals.

### 2.4 How much unstructuredness compiles away

Almost all of it. Of the six runtime carriers (Part I Section 7), fragmented lines become per-point interior bits plus closure rows; irregular R lengths become segments of one packed vector; psi-dependent closures become rows of a padded ELL table evaluated once; the four CSRs B/N/Bf/Br become one compact exception table, which also removes the full-height |D|+1 int64 row pointers that B and N sweep on every call (`src/matrices/csr.cpp:37-51`, 134 MB each per derivative at 256^3); gather lists become one byte of role bits per point; partial-coverage writes become a total output. Counts: the 256^3 sphere has about 78.3k lines per direction (19.5% of pencils fragmented), about 400k closure rows per direction (2.4% of D) and R work at 0.46% of D, leaving about 15.3M interior rows per direction (about 91% of D rows, about 97% of the about 15.7M fluid rows). The three setup-only carriers (the `interp()` dispatch, the `interp_line` scan, the bit-exact ray casting) stay on the host; this holds only for static geometry.

One real hazard: the per-direction R ordering is an implicit contract shared by field buffers, B columns, Bf/Br rows and `object_coordinate` (`src/mesh/object_geometry.cpp:51-114`, `src/mesh/mesh.cpp:64-73`); a one-ulp difference in a ported ray caster silently permutes R and corrupts every table, so the hybrid stores a checksum (D4's idea).

## 4. Recommended path

### 4.1 What is built

The Part III hybrid, working name `pyshoccs`, four pieces (Part III 9.1, which carries its 51-idiom coverage table):

1. A C++ table exporter, `ccs::assemble::derivative_tables`, keeping the loop bodies of `domain_discretization` (`src/operators/derivative.cpp:305-449`) and `cut_discretization` (`derivative.cpp:159-257`) and changing only the sinks. It emits the exact operator as COO over the packed vector (the scipy export and the oracle), taps, role bits, wall slabs, one sorted exception table and an R-ordering checksum.
2. A nanobind module `_shoccs` exposing mesh, stencils, BCs, tables, the legacy `derivative` apply as a frozen oracle, the backend API and DLPack views.
3. A `ccs::backend` on Kokkos host with ten primitives: D5's B1 to B10 with B4 (`face_closure`) absorbed into B3's slab form, plus B11, the fused `rk_stage` (an axis stencil whose epilogue does the RK axpy pair, the hoisted source add, the Dirichlet fill and stats partials). One RK4 step becomes about 12 to 16 nodes, K steps per native call.
4. A Python package `shoccs` (NumPy plus SymPy) replacing Lua, with `Operator.to_scipy()` feeding the stability cascade and the BO loop in process.

Not built now: a tracing front end, a kernel IR with emitters, a Python-AST language. The one place a DSL is needed is pointwise physics, and `StencilCodePrinter` (`scripts/stencil_gen/stencil_gen/printer.py:11`) already prints SymPy as C++.

Adjustment after Part IV D, stated as an adjustment and not a new design: phase 1 gains a parallel array arm. The real exported tables from piece 1 run through the `experiments/host_primitives_bench.py` harness with NumPy, compiled MLX CPU and torch.compile CPU, alongside the C++ fused-stage arm; gate (2) applies to both arms. If the array arm meets the discretization bar with real tables, piece 3 (the Kokkos CPU backend) becomes optional and the thin waist shrinks to C++ assembly plus nanobind export plus a Python runtime. The synthetic Part IV D result that motivates the arm is not a pass of the gate.

### 4.2 Phases

Durations and gates are stated identically in Part III Section 9.2, which also carries the full content of each phase. "Fixed-up C++" means the four frozen changes of Section 1. "Clamp-adjacent" rows are closure rows whose psi lies below 1e-4 or above 1 - 1e-4 (the E4_1 clamp bounds, the tightest in the code); they get tol = 64 eps x max|coef| x h or an exact-rational reference to 4 ulp. R rows are judged against the monomial oracle (residual <= 16 eps x sum |coef x x^k|), never the legacy C++, because `t-laplacian` shows the legacy Bf/Br path is itself untrusted. Solid, Dirichlet and Dirichlet-skipped points are excluded (Part III 9.1(e)).

### 4.3 First de-risking experiment and go/no-go

Phase 1 needs no nanobind and no new build system: a C++ arm (compact tables plus a fused stage kernel against the live Kokkos RHS) and a Python array arm (the same exported tables through the existing `host_primitives_bench.py` harness). It answers the one question everything else depends on: does either arm match or beat the fixed-up Kokkos RHS and step on this machine, natively? Everything is measured at the thread count and toolchain fixed in phase 1(a): 1 thread for the shipped 2D cases, where 8 threads are slower than 1, and 8 threads from 201x201 up.

Go: all five phase-1 gates of Section 4.2, within the 3-working-day tuning box. No-go and what it means: the fused stage misses the 65^3 or 128^3 gates while the unfused primitives still win on launch count, so ship those and re-evaluate D1's tile-class generator with the same tables; even the unfused primitives miss at 65^3, so the plane-tiled masked-stencil thesis is wrong and the recommendation changes; the stop rule: stop at phase 2 if the native fixed-up heat.lua step is within 1.5x of four native RHS graph submits plus stats (no non-discretization overhead left to remove), or if gate (3) fails; the numeric threshold is set after phase 1(b), and Python then stays a Lua replacement and `to_scipy()` provider; the array arm passes gate (2) with real tables, so the Kokkos CPU backend becomes optional and the waist shrinks to assembly, export and a Python runtime; the monomial oracle shows the Bf/Br R-row formulation itself inexact or unstable, so fix the numerics before porting R rows; the fp32 study passes (convergence order and rightmost-eigenvalue sign preserved for E2_2, polyE2_1 and E4_2), so Metal becomes fp32-first and MLX or torch-MPS custom kernels become acceptable hosts, while if both fp32 and df64 fail phase 4 is deleted. Part III Section 10 lists further triggers.

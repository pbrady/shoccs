# Python DSL Survey for SHOCCS: Editor's Report

Status as of 2026-10-13. Branch `worktree-python-dsl-survey`, base commit 4895b5a. This report consolidates four parts, one fact sheet, one cross-cutting note and, since the sixth pass, Part V, the phase-1 results (`06-phase1.md`, measured 2026-10-13 on branch `pyshoccs-phase1`), under `docs/python-dsl-survey/` (document map in Section 8). The C++ figures quoted in Sections 1 and 2 are from the native macOS build of 2026-10-13 (Part IV E, `05-measurements.md`), with the devcontainer figures of 2026-10-12 kept beside them; every other number is taken from the parts. Section 7 says which figure was kept where the parts disagreed.

## Executive summary

The maintainer asked three questions. The short answers follow, then the recommendation.

**Which idioms must be handled efficiently?** Part I catalogues 51 idioms (I01 to I51; 29 in the time loop). Every hot idiom reduces to seven kernel shapes (P0 to P6, `01-codebase-idioms.md` Section 4), none of which needs a general sparse matrix, atomics, team scratch or a library solver. Cut-cell "unstructuredness" enters the runtime in exactly six places (Section 2.4), and all six reduce at assembly time to O(|R|) tables around a regular axis stencil. At 256^3 with a sphere (16.78M points, about 6.5% solid, so about 15.7M fluid points) each direction has about 400k closure rows (2.4% of D) and about 15.3M interior rows with a shared stencil, about 91% of D rows and about 97% of fluid rows; the irregular gather-dot-scatter touches 0.5 to 2.5% of points (Part I Section 7). The only blocker is I35, the per-point Lua callback, which every option replaces with SymPy-generated array expressions.

**Which Python packages fit, and how are our idioms expressed or left out?** Part II surveyed 18 options with an independent verification pass: 164 claims after the recount (originally 161 = 11 Apple fact-sheet claims + 150 framework claims): 99 confirmed, 7 refuted, 58 uncertain; 244 including the nine comparators (144/12/88), which were added in this pass (Section 3). No option scores well on both the fp64 CPU bar and Apple GPU: the best CPU candidates (Numba 9, Devito 8.5, DaCe, Halide and Mojo 8) score 0 to 4 on Apple GPU, and the best Apple access (direct Metal 8, MLX 6.5) has no fp64 CPU path. All express our idioms the same way (Part II Section 5) and leave out the same eight things (Section 6), among them the Lua callback and Apple fp64. The shortlist is a set of roles: Numba (loop-form CPU emitter), JAX (array-form CPU/CUDA emitter; with Devito one of the two options whose local small-2D measurements fall below the C++ figure, Devito's being serial and against an estimate), NumPy/SciPy assembled CSR (oracle and table producer), direct Metal (fp32 or df64, gated on a precision study). PyTorch, Halide, Devito and DaCe are second tier. A host measurement made on 2026-10-12 (Part IV D, a synthetic SHOCCS-shaped RK4 step on the owner's M4) adds the datapoint the survey lacked, and since the native C++ measurement of 2026-10-13 (Part IV E) both sides of the comparison come from the same machine. A fused fp64 step on the CPU from Python (eager NumPy 0.10 ms, compiled MLX CPU 0.085 ms, torch eager 0.15 ms at 51x51) sits at the discretization bar of four native one-thread C++ RHS submits (84 us; 1.0x to 1.8x) and far under the shipped step (0.856 ms natively at 1 thread, 0.266 ms with four spin-waiting threads; container bar 91 us and 0.90 ms); at 65^3 the best Python CPU fp64 paths (4.2 to 4.5 ms) are 1.1x to 1.2x of four native four-thread Kokkos RHS submits (3.8 ms) and about half the one-thread figure (8.3 ms; container 3.2 and 7.5 ms), so CPU parity is plausible but not yet shown in 3D or with real coefficients. The Apple GPU in fp32 is held at 0.35 to 0.5 ms per step by a 167 to 181 us sync floor at the shipped 2D sizes, where it loses to the CPU, and is 4 to 6x faster than the best CPU fp64 path at 129^3 and 257^3.

**What would it take to build our own DSL?** Part III developed five designs to one template and had three judges score them. Each states its effort in its own unit and to its own endpoint, so the figures are not comparable: D3 6 to 8 calendar months (Euler-capable, CPU and Metal), D2 7 to 8 months (CPU, Metal, Euler, CUDA), D5 7 to 9 developer-months (Metal and Euler), D1 8 to 12 person-months (its phase 4 only, not Euler), D4 10 to 13 engineer-months (Euler on CPU plus Metal df64), plus 10 to 25% of an FTE ongoing for the compiler designs. D5 is not the low end of that range; the judges still ranked it, the no-compiler "thin waist", first unanimously (171 of 225; the other four lie within 6.5 points, inside rubric noise), because its central claim can be tested in weeks of C++ against the live Kokkos RHS.

**Recommendation.** Adopt the Part III hybrid (`03-dsl-designs.md` Section 9): keep the C++ mesh, stencils and assembly loops; change only the assembly sinks to export compact tables and the exact operator over a packed vector; add a nanobind module and a Kokkos backend of ten primitives (B1 to B11, B4 absorbed into B3; B labels are backend primitives, P0 to P6 Part I's kernel shapes), one of them the fused RK stage kernel B11; replace Lua with a NumPy plus SymPy front end; build no compiler now. Treat Apple GPU as a gated phase 4 in fp32 or hand-written double-float, because no Apple GPU has fp64 and no framework offers an emulated dtype (Part IV D measures the fp32 GPU at 0.35 to 0.5 ms per synthetic step at the shipped sizes against 0.085 ms on the CPU, and 4 to 6x ahead of the CPU at 129^3 and 257^3). The C++ measured natively (Section 1, Part IV E) puts the shipped heat.lua step at 0.856 ms at 1 thread and 0.266 ms with four spin-waiting threads, 28 to 37% of the one-thread step being host manufactured-solution evaluation in fill_source, against an RHS graph of 21.0 us (container: 0.90 ms, 59%, 22.7 us); every design compared itself against that inflated bar, and the fixed-up step is estimated at 131 us (container estimate about 0.15 ms). Adjustment after the host measurement: phase 1 gains a parallel array arm (the real exported tables through the `host_primitives_bench.py` harness with NumPy, compiled MLX CPU and torch.compile CPU, under the same gate (2) as the C++ fused-stage arm); if it passes with real tables, the Kokkos CPU backend becomes optional and the thin waist shrinks to C++ assembly plus nanobind export plus a Python runtime. The native build has existed since 2026-10-13; the most valuable next action is to measure the fixed-up C++ on it (phase 1(b)).

The shipped step is not a fair bar, because 28 to 37% of the native one-thread step at 51x51 is host fill_source (59% in the container; Section 2.2), so the bar is the fixed-up C++, frozen as exactly four changes: (1) hoist the object-BC gather lists out of the per-call path; (2) compact B and N tables with no full-height row pointers; (3) manufactured-solution evaluation once per step and only where needed, with time factors hoisted (fill_source and update_boundary); (4) packed [D | Rx | Ry | Rz] buffers. The fixed-up rows model change (3) only and are estimates: they keep the RHS submit and stats and replace fill_source and update_boundary by a residual of 5 us per stage at 51x51 and 0.3 ms per stage at 65^3. At 4t spin the stage micro-benchmarks overstate the in-step cost (Part IV E: 570 us of stage work against a 0.266 ms step), so there is no decomposed estimate there; the fixed-up 4t-spin step lies between the one-thread estimate of 131 us and the measured 0.266 ms. The thread count per case is fixed by Part IV E: 1 thread for the shipped 2D cases, where four threads with libomp defaults are slower than one (RHS 309 us against 21.0 us at 51x51) and even spinning threads lose on the RHS submit alone (54.1 us), and 4 threads from 201x201 up, spin-waiting below 65^3 and libomp defaults at 65^3 for the RHS submit (961 against 1810 us); that is the better of the two settings per case, as Part IV E puts it, and the shipped 65^3 step itself is still fastest spinning (17.49 against 19.96 ms), while at 201x201 one thread still wins the submit alone (217 against 228 us) and spinning wins the step (2.17 against 6.82 ms); a serial path for small cases is therefore part of the bar. Regime C (256^3) remains unmeasured in C++: about 50 ms per RHS at 8 threads and about 115 ms at 1 thread, scaled from the container 65^3 figures, with a bandwidth floor of 16 to 26 ms (Part I 6.4 L1). The synthetic Python step is measured there: 272 ms CPU fp64 and 43.6 ms GPU fp32 at 257^3 (Part IV D).

## 2. Findings that constrain the decision

### 2.1 fp64 on Apple GPUs

No Apple GPU (M1 to M5) has hardware fp64 and MSL has no usable `double`; PyTorch MPS 2.14.1 raises on float64; MLX runs fp64 on CPU only (request #1905 closed as not planned); jax-metal is dead; Taichi's and Halide's Metal backends have no f64; Kokkos 5.x has no Metal backend; AdaptiveCpp's SYCL-to-Metal backend is experimental, macOS 26 only, no `double`. All confirmed in the `00-apple-gpu-facts.md` log.

Consequences. "MPS like PyTorch" cannot mean fp64 for this solver; the options are fp32 or a hand-written double-float (df64) layer, which no framework offers as a dtype. Whether fp32 is accurate and stable for small-psi closures is open; psi is clamped to [1e-12, 1-1e-12] at `src/mesh/object_geometry.cpp:87-95` and E4_1 further clamps to [1e-4, 1-1e-4]. Part III Section 8.5 adds a point the designs missed: the shipped workloads (BO sweeps, stability scans) are ensembles of small 2D cases, not the 128^3 to 256^3 runs the designs optimise for, so a GPU pays here only by batching many independent small cases per dispatch, which nobody has measured with real tables; phase 4 carries an ensemble gate for it. Part IV D supplies the floor that gate has to amortise: on the M4 any GPU op plus synchronisation costs 167 to 181 us, so a whole-step-compiled fp32 step is 0.35 to 0.5 ms from 51x51 to 201x201 against 0.085 ms for the best CPU fp64 path at 51x51, and the GPU leads clearly only from 401x401 (0.57 against 1.49 ms). The fp32 error on the synthetic E2 problem is 1e-7 to 2.4e-7 relative after 10 steps; it says nothing about real small-psi closures.

### 2.2 Dispatch overhead at small sizes

At heat.lua scale the state is L2-resident and every kernel is launch-bound. The measured C++ launch floor is 0.28 us per tiny kernel at 1 thread on the native build (0.55 us in the container); a step issues about 280 launches (Part I Section 5.2). The native shipped step at 51x51 on 1 thread decomposes as 4 x (21.0 us RHS graph + 59.8 us fill_source + 24.4 us update_boundary) + 26.9 us stats = 448 us of a 0.65 to 0.86 ms step (median 0.856 ms, minimum 0.648, p90 1.35: a lone busy thread is not pinned to a performance core, so the one-thread step is noisy while the stage micro-benchmarks are not); the remainder, 0.2 to 0.4 ms, is slot copies, timestep logic, logging and the write call. So fill_source is 28 to 37%, update_boundary 11 to 15%, the four RHS submits 10 to 13%, stats 3 to 4% (`05-measurements.md` Section E). In the container the same decomposition was 4 x (22.7 + 132 + 33.9) + 31.9 = 786 us of a 0.90 ms step, with fill_source at 59%, update_boundary 15%, the four submits 10% and stats 4% (Section C); host fill_source is 2.2x cheaper natively.

So any Python route must compile the whole RK step, or K steps, into a handful of kernels with one readback per chunk. The two local whole-step figures below the C++ figures are JAX (step 66 to 74 us at 51x51; 1.7 ms at 64^3; 86 ms at 256^3) and Devito (7.2 us per fused step at 51x51, serial, against an estimate on synthetic tables); both were taken on the Mac host while the C++ still ran in the container, and both must now be read against the native fixed-up estimate of 131 us at 1 thread (container estimate about 0.15 ms), not the shipped step (0.856 ms natively at 1 thread, 0.266 ms at 4t spin; 0.90 ms in the container). Part IV D measures the same shape of step on the same host, and since Part IV E the C++ bar is on that machine too (Section 1 table): 0.085 to 0.149 ms per synthetic fused fp64 CPU step at 51x51 against 84 us for four native 1-thread RHS submits (1.0x to 1.8x) and 216 us at 4t spin (0.4x to 0.7x; container: 91 us); 4.2 to 4.5 ms per synthetic step at 65^3 against 3.8 ms (4 x 961 us at 4 threads; 1.1x to 1.2x) and 8.3 ms (1 thread; about 0.5x) of native submits (container: 3.2 ms at 8 threads, 7.5 ms at 1). Measured dispatch floors: 0.46 us (NumPy), 0.75 us (torch eager), 6.4 us (torch.compile CPU), 8.7 us (MLX CPU compiled), 167 to 181 us for any GPU op plus sync; eager MPS, at about a hundred kernels per step, costs 1.5 to 2.2 ms. CPU parity from Python is plausible, not yet shown in 3D or with real coefficients; the Python figures are synthetic (E2 interior plus random closure coefficients, no host MMS, no update_boundary).

### 2.3 Setup versus runtime

Everything that depends on psi, BC type, wall-versus-object or interpolation is baked into coefficients once per (geometry, BCs, scheme, alpha); the runtime inputs are u and t (Part I Section 8). Setup today is wasteful at scale (derived estimates from Part I, not measurements): about 700k tiny Kokkos allocations at 256^3 from per-line Views (`src/matrices/block.hpp:30-94`; several per line over about 78.3k lines per direction), and about 1e9 comparisons per (r, dir) pair in the O(|R|)-per-r `interp_line` scan (`src/mesh/mesh.cpp:170-207`; about 1.3e5 `interp_line` calls per pair, each scanning up to about 12k records). All of it is fixable because setup only emits tables; psi, alpha, masks and coefficients should be runtime table arguments (graft G8) so the BO loop, which today runs the binary per parameter point through `cpp_bridge.py`, never recompiles.

Two runtime habits must go in any port, and should go in the C++ regardless: per-stage host evaluation of the manufactured solution over the whole field (about 14 full-field evaluations per step, 4 of them wasted in `update_boundary`; `src/systems/heat.cpp:296-354`), and per-call rebuild of object-BC gather lists (`src/mesh/mesh.hpp:97-115`). The first is now measured: fill_source alone is 28 to 37% of the native one-thread shipped step at 51x51 and 49% of the native four-thread step at 65^3 (container: 59% and 72% at 8 threads; Section 2.2 and Part IV E). With time-separable source terms hoisted into spatial factors (graft G6), the hot loop holds no transcendentals.

### 2.4 How much unstructuredness compiles away

Almost all of it. Of the six runtime carriers (Part I Section 7), fragmented lines become per-point interior bits plus closure rows; irregular R lengths become segments of one packed vector; psi-dependent closures become rows of a padded ELL table evaluated once; the four CSRs B/N/Bf/Br become one compact exception table, which also removes the full-height |D|+1 int64 row pointers that B and N sweep on every call (`src/matrices/csr.cpp:37-51`, 134 MB each per derivative at 256^3); gather lists become one byte of role bits per point; partial-coverage writes become a total output. Counts: the 256^3 sphere has about 78.3k lines per direction (19.5% of pencils fragmented), about 400k closure rows per direction (2.4% of D) and R work at 0.46% of D, leaving about 15.3M interior rows per direction (about 91% of D rows, about 97% of the about 15.7M fluid rows). The three setup-only carriers (the `interp()` dispatch, the `interp_line` scan, the bit-exact ray casting) stay on the host; this holds only for static geometry.

One real hazard: the per-direction R ordering is an implicit contract shared by field buffers, B columns, Bf/Br rows and `object_coordinate` (`src/mesh/object_geometry.cpp:51-114`, `src/mesh/mesh.cpp:64-73`); a one-ulp difference in a ported ray caster silently permutes R and corrupts every table, so the hybrid stores a checksum (D4's idea).

## 4. Recommended path

### 4.1 What is built

The Part III hybrid, working name `pyshoccs`, four pieces (Part III 9.1, which carries its 51-idiom coverage table):

1. A C++ table exporter, `ccs::assemble::derivative_tables`, keeping the loop bodies of `domain_discretization` (`src/operators/derivative.cpp:305-449`) and `cut_discretization` (`derivative.cpp:159-257`) and changing only the sinks. It emits the exact operator as COO over the packed vector (the scipy export and the oracle), taps, role bits, wall slabs, one sorted exception table and an R-ordering checksum.
2. A nanobind module `_shoccs` exposing mesh, stencils, BCs, tables, the legacy `derivative` apply as a frozen oracle, the backend API and DLPack views.
3. A `ccs::backend` on Kokkos host with ten primitives: D5's B1 to B10 with B4 (`face_closure`) absorbed into B3's slab form, plus B11, the fused `rk_stage` (an axis stencil whose epilogue does the RK axpy pair, the hoisted source add, the Dirichlet fill and stats partials). One RK4 step becomes about 12 to 16 nodes, K steps per native call.
4. A Python package `shoccs` (NumPy plus SymPy) replacing Lua, with `Operator.to_scipy()` feeding the stability cascade and the BO loop in process.

Not built now: a tracing front end, a kernel IR with emitters, a Python-AST language. The one place a DSL is needed is pointwise physics, and `StencilCodePrinter` (`scripts/stencil_gen/stencil_gen/printer.py:11`) already prints SymPy as C++.

Adjustment after Part IV D, stated as an adjustment and not a new design: phase 1 gains a parallel array arm. The real exported tables from piece 1 run through the `experiments/host_primitives_bench.py` harness with NumPy, compiled MLX CPU and torch.compile CPU, alongside the C++ fused-stage arm; gate (2) applies to both arms. If the array arm meets the discretization bar with real tables, piece 3 (the Kokkos CPU backend) becomes optional and the thin waist shrinks to C++ assembly plus nanobind export plus a Python runtime. The synthetic Part IV D result that motivates the arm is not a pass of the gate; against the native bar of Part IV E it is 1.0x to 1.8x of four 1-thread RHS submits at 51x51 (84 us) and 1.1x to 1.2x of the 4-thread submits at 65^3 (3.8 ms). ### 4.3 First de-risking experiment and go/no-go

Phase 1 needs no nanobind and no new build system: a C++ arm (compact tables plus a fused stage kernel against the live Kokkos RHS) and a Python array arm (the same exported tables through the existing `host_primitives_bench.py` harness). It answers the one question everything else depends on: does either arm match or beat the fixed-up Kokkos RHS and step on this machine, natively? Everything is measured at the thread count and toolchain fixed in phase 1(a), now known from Part IV E: Apple clang 21 with Kokkos OpenMP (llvm-openmp); 1 thread for the shipped 2D cases, where four threads with libomp defaults are slower than one (RHS 309 us against 21.0 us at 51x51) and spin-waiting threads win only end to end (step 0.266 against 0.856 ms) and not on the RHS submit (54.1 us); 4 threads from 201x201 up, spin-waiting below 65^3 and libomp defaults at 65^3 for the RHS submit (961 against 1810 us), the better of the two settings per case (the 65^3 step itself is still fastest spinning, 17.49 against 19.96 ms).

Go: all five phase-1 gates of Section 4.2, within the 3-working-day tuning box. No-go and what it means: the fused stage misses the 65^3 or 128^3 gates while the unfused primitives still win on launch count, so ship those and re-evaluate D1's tile-class generator with the same tables; even the unfused primitives miss at 65^3, so the plane-tiled masked-stencil thesis is wrong and the recommendation changes; the stop rule: stop at phase 2 if the native fixed-up heat.lua step is within 1.5x of four native RHS graph submits plus stats (no non-discretization overhead left to remove), or if gate (3) fails; the numeric threshold is set after phase 1(b), and Python then stays a Lua replacement and `to_scipy()` provider. Restated from Part IV E, the rule reads: at 51x51 and 1 thread four submits plus stats are 4 x 21.0 + 26.9 = 111 us, the threshold is 166 us and the modelled fixed-up step is 131 us (1.18x); at 65^3 and 4 threads with libomp defaults they are 4 x 961 + 955 = 4.8 ms, the threshold 7.2 ms and the estimate 6.0 ms (1.25x). The rule fires in both cases if the estimate holds. That means: once change (3) is made, the C++ step is almost entirely its discretization, so phases 2 and later cannot be justified by removing host overhead; they rest on gate (2), beating the native RHS graph itself (21.0 us at 1 thread and 54 us at 4 threads spin at 51x51; 961 us at 4 threads at 65^3), on the Python front end and `to_scipy()`, and on the ensemble GPU question of phase 4. The rule is judged after phase 1(b), when the four changes are measured, not on this estimate; the array arm passes gate (2) with real tables, so the Kokkos CPU backend becomes optional and the waist shrinks to assembly, export and a Python runtime; the monomial oracle shows the Bf/Br R-row formulation itself inexact or unstable, so fix the numerics before porting R rows; the fp32 study passes (convergence order and rightmost-eigenvalue sign preserved for E2_2, polyE2_1 and E4_2), so Metal becomes fp32-first and MLX or torch-MPS custom kernels become acceptable hosts, while if both fp32 and df64 fail phase 4 is deleted. Part III Section 10 lists further triggers.

Outcome (Part V, `06-phase1.md`, 2026-10-13). Gate (1): passed on the three real exports (heat.lua 51x51, 201x201, the 65^3 sphere): the operator, RHS and step identities within 5e-16 of the compared vector's maximum (check 2 on `u0_V`, whose maximum is small at t0, within 0.05 of its per-row tolerance), the axial oracle exact through k = 2 on every R row, and the structural decomposition equal between the two arms (bitwise, up to two signed zeros at 201x201). Gate (2): the C++ fused-stage arm at 0.13x of the shipped graph submit at 51x51 and 1 thread and 0.12x at 65^3 and 4 threads, every launch form under the thresholds; the array arm at 0.76x at 51x51 (scipy CSR, the only backend under 1.0x) and 0.78x at 65^3 (torch.compile, against a 0.5x threshold), so met by the C++ arm, not met by the array arm at 65^3, and the Kokkos CPU backend stays. Gate (3): the fused step at 0.21x of a graph-built step with the source and boundary vectors held fixed (93.7 us) at 51x51 and 1 thread, 0.10x at 4 threads spin, 0.20x at 65^3 and 4 threads. Gate (4): not measured (no 128^3 case); the STREAM triad it is a fraction of is 90.3 GiB/s at one thread on 134 MB buffers. Gate (5): computed from the heat3d65 export, compact 1.84 MB against legacy 1.89 MB, 0.975x, met by 2.5 percent. Every "met" is provisional because the gates name the fixed-up C++ of phase 1(b), which does not exist yet and can only be faster; the "not met" stands. The stop rule fires on a measured proxy for change (3): the graph-built step plus stats is 93.7 + 26.9 = 120.6 us at 51x51 and 1 thread, 1.09x of the 111 us base (the estimate said 1.18x), and 5.4 ms at 65^3 and 4 threads, 1.13x of 4.8 ms; the fused compact-table step, 20.1 us, is 0.18x of the base and the only route found below the discretization bar. The monomial oracle finds the transverse R rows of the E2 order-2 export zeroth-order inconsistent at the row level (Part V Section 3), the trigger this section names for fixing the numerics before porting R rows; it is a numerics decision for the maintainer, not a result of either arm.
